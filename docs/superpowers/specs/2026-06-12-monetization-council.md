## STAGE 1 — THE FIVE ADVISORS

### ADVISOR 1 — THE CONTRARIAN

This fails on money for one structural reason: **your honesty law deletes the purchase trigger.** Hone built a real subscription business selling "more FPS." Compy is constitutionally forbidden from making that promise. The category's revenue is funded by hope; you sell the absence of hope. Nobody impulse-buys "your PC is probably fine."

Worse, the product argues against its own subscription: 29 frozen recs, a library full of debunks, and most rigs come back "already fine." The better Compy works, the faster a user is *done* with it. That's a one-and-done utility wearing subscription clothes — built-in churn.

Free incumbents surround you: Windows Game Mode, AMD Adrenalin, the NVIDIA App, Razer Cortex — preinstalled or vendor-pushed, all $0.

Distribution is zero. An unsigned exe via a Google Drive link in DMs — MalwareBytes already false-blocked your kit once; SmartScreen will execute your paid funnel at the door. Solo owner, no audience, no channel. And no telemetry means you can't measure actives, conversion, or retention — you'd run a business on vibes, which is ironic for a measurement app.

Most likely failure mode: not product failure. **Obscurity.** Compy dies with 200 users who all loved it.

### ADVISOR 2 — THE FIRST PRINCIPLES THINKER

The question smuggles in a false unit: "Compy the app" is not the asset. Decompose what actually exists: (1) a **typed rollback engine** for Windows config — genuinely rare; (2) a **measurement harness** (ETW FPS, receipts); (3) a **~120-entry honest content corpus**; (4) a **trust posture** (laws, local-only); (5) an **AI-relay dev process** that produces audited software at near-zero marginal cost. The app is one packaging of five assets.

People pay when pain is acute, recurring, and they believe the fix. Walk the pains: ping — acute, measurable, gamers pay ExitLag $3.29/mo. FPS envy — acute, gamers pay Hone on a promise. Fear-of-breaking-things and conflicting-advice fatigue — real, but consumers under-pay for prevention. Insurance is sold, not bought.

Who feels Compy's exact value as acute pain? **People responsible for OTHER people's machines** — repair shops, boutique builders, the family "tech person," cafés. For them, rollback + receipts = liability reduction = a line item, not a vibe.

So the real question is not "what is Compy worth?" It is "**who pays for certainty about a Windows machine's config state?**" Consumers: rarely. Businesses: routinely. And note: with the AI relay your cost base is ~$0, so define the bar — venture, side income, or craft — before measuring against it. The question as framed has no answer until the bar exists.

### ADVISOR 3 — THE EXPANSIONIST

You're sitting on a media company locked inside an exe. The Library — ~120 honest entries — answers exactly the questions millions of gamers type into search ("does disabling HPET boost FPS?"). Inside the app it has zero acquisition value. Published on compy.gg it's an SEO/debunk trust funnel: **the Snopes of PC tweaks.** Trust media in this space prints money at scale — Gamers Nexus, RTINGS — via membership and affiliate.

Every export share-card and overlay screenshot is a free ad. "Post your Compy report" as a r/buildapc ritual is an organic loop nobody has to buy.

The bigger version: Compy is a **config provenance layer**. The Carfax for gaming PCs — used-PC sales need a trusted health report; repair shops need an intake/triage receipt; boutique builders could ship every rig with a Compy report. The honesty that weakens consumer conversion is *exactly* the feature B2B buyers pay for.

And the build itself is content: an AI-relay-built app with 300+ tests, with stories like Compy's own webview out-presenting the game it was measuring — that's build-in-public gold, free distribution a solo owner can compound. ExitLag proves gamers pay monthly for **measured** value. Sell receipts, not hope — the upside is a trust brand, and trust brands outlive tweak fads.

### ADVISOR 4 — THE OUTSIDER

I know nothing about PC optimization, so here's what looks weird:

You built a product for self-described non-technical, safety-anxious people… and you deliver it as an **unsigned executable from a Google Drive link in a DM.** That is the exact shape of every scam your target user has been warned about their whole life. Your distribution method is your persona's nightmare.

Your pitch is "most tweaks don't work; your PC is probably fine." Then why am I downloading a tweak app? You're selling a vaccine while announcing the disease is fake. From outside, this isn't a hot-rod product — it's a **health-records product wearing a hot-rod costume.**

The asymmetry is bizarre: 300+ automated tests, cryptographically triple-gated update rails, typed rollback contracts — and **no landing page, no analytics, no pricing, no waitlist.** A Ferrari engine on a bench: no fuel gauge, no wheels, no road.

Insiders assume "gamers" is the market. The persona you describe — afraid of breaking the family PC, drowning in conflicting YouTube advice — is equally parents, office workers, and small repair shops. The gamer skin might be the costume, not the body. And "no telemetry" worn as a badge while asking "what's my revenue potential" — even privacy-first companies count installs. You can't run a shop with the lights off.

### ADVISOR 5 — THE EXECUTOR

You don't have a monetization problem yet. You have a "**do strangers want this**" problem, and it's testable by Friday for $0.

Smallest shippable thing: a one-page compy.gg site — three Library debunks published as pages, one screenshot, an email waitlist. No app changes, no cert, no payments, no business plan.

This week: post ONE build-in-public story — the FPS self-measurement bug (Compy's own webview out-presenting the game, caught and fixed with receipts) is the hook — to r/pcgaming or X, linked to the site.

One metric: **waitlist signups in 14 days.** Zero signups = you bought the answer to the monetary-potential question for free. A hundred-plus = you've earned the pricing conversation. Everything else — certs, EULAs, B2B decks, subscription tiers — is procrastination dressed as strategy. Ship the page.

## STAGE 3 — THE CHAIRMAN

**Recommendation:** As a consumer-paid app, Compy is hobby-tier revenue — and that's structural, not a polish problem. Its honesty law forbids the promise (more FPS) that funds this category, and it has zero distribution. So don't price the app. Reposition the assets: publish the Library to compy.gg as the acquisition engine; keep the app free as the trust engine; aim future revenue at people responsible for *other people's* PCs — repair shops, boutique builders, used-PC health checks — where rollback + receipts is a paid feature, not a vibe. ExitLag proves gamers pay monthly for measured value; Hone proves promise-sellers own the consumer lane; a competitor is already marketing "rollback-aware." The window for "the honest one" is open, but not forever.

**The single most important reason:** distribution, not product, is the binding constraint — 300+ tests, ~0 audience. Until strangers arrive on their own, every pricing debate is theater.

**One concrete step today:** put three Library debunks and an email waitlist on compy.gg, post the FPS-bug build-in-public story once, and measure 14 days of signups.
